Bulk beef is one of the best things a small farm can sell. One customer, one sale, a whole freezer full. It's also one of the hardest things to explain on a website, because the final price depends on a weight nobody knows yet. Here's how to set it up so customers understand it and you're not answering the same questions all week.

## Explain the pricing in plain words

Most farms price bulk beef by hanging weight, which is the weight of the carcass after slaughter, before it's cut and wrapped. The beef that ends up in the freezer weighs less than the hanging weight, often around 60 to 65 percent of it, depending on the animal and the cuts chosen.

Customers don't know this, and it's the number one source of confusion. Put a short explanation right on the page:

- The price per pound of hanging weight
- Whether processing is included or paid separately to the processor
- A rough range for what a quarter, half, and whole usually weigh and cost from your farm
- About how much freezer space each one needs

A simple example ("A half from us usually runs about X pounds hanging and fills about 8 cubic feet of freezer") answers more questions than a paragraph of explanation.

## Take a deposit online

You don't know the final price at checkout, so don't try to charge it. Sell a deposit instead. In Shopify, Square, or a farm platform like Barn2Door or GrazeCart, set up one product for each share size, priced at the deposit amount. Then send the balance as an invoice once you have the hanging weight.

Say plainly on the product page what the deposit covers, when the balance is due, and whether the deposit is refundable.

## Show your processing dates

Your processor dates drive everything. If you have slots booked for March, June, and September, let customers pick one at checkout and set the inventory for each date to the number of shares you have. When a date is full, it shows as sold out and people can choose the next one. That alone saves a lot of back-and-forth.

## Make the cut sheet easy

First-time buyers find cut sheets intimidating. Link to your processor's sheet if they have one online, or write a short "most popular choices" version for people who just want a good mix. Tell them who to call with questions, you or the processor.

## Follow up after the beef is picked up

A week or two after pickup, send a short email: how to thaw a roast, a recipe or two, and a line about when your next dates open. Bulk buyers who were happy tend to buy again, but only if you remind them when it's time. Ask for a Google review in the same email, too.

## Keep the website current

Nothing loses a sale faster than a "half beef available" page that's been sold out since spring. When shares sell out, say when the next ones open and offer a waitlist signup so you keep those people.
